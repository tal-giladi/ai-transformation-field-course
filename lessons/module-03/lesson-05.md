# 03.5 · Running the scoring workshop

Your own scores are an opinion with a spreadsheet. The organization's scores are a decision its leaders will defend. The scoring workshop turns the first into the second: the people who own value, money, risk and the work score the candidates themselves, under criteria they already agreed, and leave owning the result. In this lesson you write the facilitator script, run the workshop with four Harbor & Pine leaders in a group roleplay, and send the read-back the same afternoon.

## The situation

Thursday 26 November 2026, 10:00. You locked the criteria with Michal, Ronit and Samir on Tuesday ([03.4](lesson-04.md)) and sent the fast screen ([03.3](lesson-03.md)) two days ago. Around the table: Yoav, who announced the chatbot to the board and has a call at 10:40; Michal, your sponsor, who will not contradict him in public; Ronit, who wants to know how every number was measured; and Samir, who runs the team whose work changes and who says little in front of the CEO. ChatNest's early-signing discount expires on Monday.

IBM's leadership certificate warns about the "negotiation trap" (selection by the loudest sponsor) and recommends scoring independently and discussing divergence, not averages, but never shows how to run the room. This lesson is the room.

## What to do

### Step 1 — Design the session backwards from its one output

Write the output first: "the three candidates that go to the alignment session on 10 December, each with its conditions, and what is parked and why". Then the agenda that produces it. Ninety minutes:

| Minutes | Block | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | Purpose and output | Say what is decided today and what is not (no money) |
| 5–12 | Criteria read-back | One participant states the locked criteria with the sheet closed |
| 12–20 | What the screen stopped | Reasons in one line each; reopened only with new evidence |
| 20–30 | Independent scoring | Everyone scores the cards on their own sheet, in silence |
| 30–60 | Divergence | Only cards with a spread of 2 or more; evidence, not opinion |
| 60–75 | Absorption and sequence | Each team's number; what fits when |
| 75–85 | Read-back | The three, the conditions, owners and dates; "did I miss anything?" |
| 85–90 | Actions | Who does what by when; the read-back email today |

If the most senior person leaves early, move what needs them to the start. Yoav leaves at 10:40: the criteria read-back and his scores must happen before then.

### Step 2 — Prepare the room

- **Pre-read two days ahead:** the locked criteria with anchors, the screen table, one page per candidate that passed (the scorecard part A). Proposers of stopped ideas have heard it from you privately already.
- **Score sheets:** one per person, the cards in rows, value / feasibility / risk in columns, the anchors at the top. Paper or a form; never a shared screen where people see each other's scores as they type.
- **Your numbers:** for each card, the evidence line and the net benefit after errors, so you can answer "how was that measured?" in one sentence.
- **Absorption:** ask the team leads before the meeting (Tamar, Yaniv) and bring their numbers; ask the one in the room (Samir) again in the room.
- **A parking lot:** a visible list for anything off the agenda, with an owner.

### Step 3 — Read the criteria back, and hold them

Ask someone other than you to state them. If the CEO proposes a change ("give the board's priority a bonus"), do not argue about the chatbot; argue about the order of decisions: "If we change the rules after we've seen the list, the board will ask whether we set the rules to get the answer. I'll score it fairly and show you why it lands where it lands." Offer something real in return: you will show him card by card, and the evidence can move it.

### Step 4 — Scores first, talk second

Independent scoring before discussion is the single most important move. If the CEO speaks first about his card, everyone drifts toward his number (the pre-run transcript shows it: Michal echoes "it's a strong story" before anyone scores). Collect the sheets, write the scores in a table everyone can see, and **do not average them**. Circle the cards with a spread of two points or more on any dimension: that is where someone has evidence the others lack.

### Step 5 — Discuss divergence as evidence

For each circled card, ask the lowest and the highest scorer the same question: "What are you looking at that the others might not be?" Then let the evidence decide.

- Ask by name, junior first. Samir will not volunteer his view of the chatbot in front of the CEO; asked directly, he gives the most important sentence of the morning: the bot "would just say the wrong date, nicely, at night".
- Translate positions into conditions. Samir's risk 3 on the drafts (Arabic and Russian quality, fear of cuts) becomes two written conditions: a weekly native-speaker sample, and no automatic sending. He moves to 2 himself.
- Keep people out of it. When someone says "fewer people", the sponsor or you close it at once ("nobody in this room is talking about people"). If you let it run, Samir stops talking for the rest of the session.

### Step 6 — Handle the favourite idea without a public defeat

The CEO's chatbot will rank near the bottom. In the room your job is to let the criteria and the evidence say so, not you, and to leave the CEO a way to agree later:

- Show the numbers, not a verdict: what the vendor's 70% was measured on, what the bot would tell a WISMO customer, the error cost.
- Put the pressure in proportion. The 15% early-signing discount is about US$217 a month, roughly ₪9,700 a year at the finance planning rate of ₪3.70 per US$; signing commits a 12-month minimum term (about ₪64,000 in platform fees) plus ₪85,000 implementation, for the card that scores last.
- Do not ask him to give it up in front of his team. Ask for one thing he can own ("bring me the plan on the 10th") and book the one-to-one where the real conversation happens (lesson [03.9](lesson-09.md) teaches it).

### Step 7 — Read back, then write it down today

Read back the result in the room: the three candidates, each with its condition, owner and date; what is parked and why; the actions. Ask "Did I miss anything?" and write what they add. Send it by email the same afternoon, in the same words. The read-back is what the alignment session starts from.

## Watch out

- **Letting the CEO open the discussion.** Everyone anchors on the first confident number. Scores first.
- **Averaging.** An average of 1 and 5 is a 3 that nobody believes. The spread is the information.
- **Arguing with a person instead of a criterion.** "Your chatbot is a bad idea" makes it personal; "under the rule we agreed, a vendor figure counts half" does not.
- **Reopening the screen.** Every stopped idea has a reopening condition. Without new evidence, it goes to the parking lot.
- **Silence read as agreement.** Samir saying nothing does not mean he agrees; ask him by name.
- **Conditions without owners.** "Staged" or "we'll check Arabic" with no owner and date will not happen. The pre-run transcript loses Ronit's condition this way.
- **No read-back email.** Two weeks later the room will remember different results. Write it the same day.

### Time

90 minutes: 25 for the script, 40 for the roleplay or the transcript, 10 for the coach, 15 for the read-back.

### Done when

- [ ] The script states one output and has an agenda with minutes in which the CEO's part comes before he leaves.
- [ ] Criteria were read back by a participant, and no rule changed during the session.
- [ ] Scores were collected independently before any discussion, and the discussion covered only cards with a spread of 2 or more.
- [ ] The quietest person was asked by name, and no one discussed headcount.
- [ ] Every condition in the read-back has an owner and a date.
- [ ] The read-back email went out the same day and names what is parked and why.
