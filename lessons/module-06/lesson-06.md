# 06.6 · Resistance conversations: the manager, the expert, the worried employee

Two days before gate 1 the numbers are on one page, and three people stand between that page and a pilot that works: a manager who believes everyone uses the tool, an expert who is the tool's best user and has quietly started fixing it herself, and an HR manager carrying the team's fear that someone is keeping a list. In this lesson you prepare and run those three conversations with Samir, Olga and Dana, by roleplay or from the pre-run transcripts, find what each one hides, and turn every conversation into dated actions. At the end you have three conversation notes, the actions agreed, and the written correction the team needs before Thursday.

## The situation

Tuesday 30 March 2027. The adoption dashboard ([06.4](lesson-04.md)) shows the drafts used steadily on email and the web form and almost not at all on WhatsApp since 22 February, when Samir set up the WhatsApp pod. The champions' log ([06.3](lesson-03.md)) has two WhatsApp issues open for five weeks. Adi Golan resigned in early March; Hila is on maternity leave. Gate 1 is on Thursday 1 April ([05.1](../module-05/lesson-01.md)), and the pre-read goes to Michal tomorrow night.

You have three meetings: Samir today at 14:00, Olga and Dana tomorrow. You could treat them as obstacles to get past before Thursday. IBM's change course says the opposite: non-use, silence and workarounds are information; check six domains before assigning a motive (role and workload; purpose and decision rights; capability and support; workflow and tool fit; quality, trust and risk; wider operating conditions); and watch the stall loop — concern, workaround, leaders read defiance, a tighter mandate, less voice. The consulting certificate adds that resistance concentrates in the middle, among experts whose standing rests on today's work, and that adoption looks better the higher you ask. Here you have the conversations.

## What to do

### Step 1 — Prepare each conversation on one card

Five lines per person, written before you walk in:

| | Samir (manager) | Olga (expert, secret enthusiast) | Dana (HR, the worried employees' channel) |
|---|---|---|---|
| What the data says | Pod since 22 Feb inserts no drafts; he has inserted none himself | Heaviest user; Russian email drafts improved; WhatsApp test set stuck at 52/100 | Adi resigned; the notice promised team-level reporting |
| What I must find out | How WhatsApp replies are written now; what he knows; what worries him about gate 1 | Why the pod stopped; what she has tried; how loaded she is | What people are saying, and where it started; who else is worried |
| What I need agreed | WhatsApp drafts off until fixed; quick replies out; how he talks about it to the team | Her fixes go through the test set; her load reduced | A correction to the team; a decision on Adi's post |
| What I can offer | No names anywhere; credit for the pod; help getting an answer on Adi | Ownership of Russian changes; Yana takes labelling; this week, not next sprint | A written who-sees-what, signed by Samir; Michal's decision asked for this week |
| What I cannot promise | That WhatsApp stays out of the pre-read | Admin rights outside change control | That Adi's job is filled |

The last row is the one that keeps you honest under pressure. Each person will push for something you cannot give; decide in advance what you say.

### Step 2 — Open with the work, then show the data without blame

Start from what the person cares about (Samir: his team; Olga: the drafts' quality; Dana: the people), then put one fact on the table that is about the work. "On WhatsApp almost no drafts are used since 22 February, and Shani logged in February that the WhatsApp drafts are six to eight lines long" is about a tool and a setup. "Three of your agents stopped using it" is about people, and the conversation is over.

### Step 3 — Listen for the domain, not the motive

For each thing you hear, ask which of the six domains it belongs to before you decide what it means:

- "It's like writing a letter to answer a text" → **workflow and tool fit**.
- "They use the quick replies" → **workflow** (an old path left open; [06.1](lesson-01.md), Step 5).
- "I stay late twice a week" → **role and workload**.
- "Give me admin rights and I'll fix it in a day" → **decision rights**.
- "I didn't want to wait to be replaced by the robot" → **purpose and trust** (and the comms pack, [06.5](lesson-05.md)).
- "Michal said let's see gate 1 first" → **wider operating conditions** (the 40% in the background).

None of these is "resistance to change". Each points at something you can fix, decide or explain.

### Step 4 — Ask the question that unlocks what they are holding

Each person holds one thing back until they feel safe and are asked the right question. Prepare it:

- **The manager:** "When the pod gets a 'where is my order' on WhatsApp, how does the reply get written?" — about the work, not about people. And: "What worries you about Thursday?" — then listen without defending the program.
- **The expert:** thank her for something specific, say nobody is in trouble, then: "Have you tried to fix the Russian drafts yourself? Did anything change in them around mid-March?"
- **HR:** "What exactly are people saying, and where did it start?" And, separately: "Is anyone who is away from work worried too?"

### Step 5 — Bring the expert's workaround into the process

The expert is the case people get wrong in both directions. Olga is your best ally, the heaviest user and a champion. If she changed a production setting outside change control, two things are true at once: her fix may be good, and nobody can now tell which change caused what, or whether she broke a flag in another language. Do not punish and do not ignore:

1. Thank her for the fix and for telling you.
2. Treat the change as a change: record it today, run the test set on the current settings this week, and compare Russian results before and after 10 March.
3. Give her what she actually wants, through the process: ownership of Russian instructions, with a weekly slot to propose changes that run through the test set, and a turnaround she can rely on.
4. Close the gap that let it happen: settings rights limited to the pilot lead and Samir, as the playbook already said; tell the team why.

### Step 6 — Agree one or two actions per conversation, dated, and close the loop

End every conversation with what each of you will do and by when, and send a three-line note the same day. Then do the things that belong to nobody in the room: ask Michal for a decision on Adi's post this week and ask her to tell the team herself; ask a senior person to write to Hila.

## Watch out

- **Arguing the data.** If Samir says "everyone is on it", do not win the argument; explain what the panel counts and ask how the work gets done. He will correct himself.
- **Promising what someone else must decide.** You cannot fill Adi's post or keep WhatsApp out of the pre-read. Say who decides and when you will ask.
- **Rewarding the workaround or punishing it.** Either teaches the team that rules are for other people or that honesty is dangerous. Bring the fix into the process and close the gap.
- **Treating HR as the messenger to be managed.** Dana carries what people do not say to you. Listen to the end before answering, and check every fact she brings.
- **Stopping at "anything else?".** A general question gets a general answer. Ask about specific groups: people on leave, the newest agent, the leavers' friends.
- **No written close.** A conversation without a dated note is forgotten by Thursday.
