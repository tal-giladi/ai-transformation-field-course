# 01.1 · Three ways in

You can walk into an AI transformation as the organization's own new lead, as an outside consultant, or as the manager who was simply told to "do AI". The work is the same; your authority, your budget, your clock and the way you leave are not. In this lesson you name your entry role, shape the engagement so it can be approved and exited, and draw the line between what you answer and what you send to a specialist. At the end you have the first page of your engagement charter and an advisory-boundary table for Harbor & Pine.

## The situation

It is 2 November 2026, your first day at Harbor & Pine Ltd., a 120-person furniture importer in Rishon LeZion. The COO, Michal Peretz, hired you as Head of AI and Operations Improvement after the CEO, Yoav Ben-Ami, told the board in September that within 90 days the company would have "an AI customer-service bot", "an AI strategy" and "40% lower support cost". The board meets again on 28 January 2027.

Before you open a single file, settle a question most people skip: **in what capacity are you here?** The same company could have brought in an outside firm for the same job, or handed it to Avi, the one-person IT department, on top of everything else. Each of those three people would need a different first week.

IBM's consulting certificate calls the commercial shape of an engagement its "form" (bounded phases you can exit, or a continuing partnership) and the line between what an advisor answers and what they route its "advisory boundary". This lesson turns both into documents you write on day one.

## What to do

### Step 1 — Name your entry role and what it changes

Write one row for yourself in this table. Be honest: most people are a mix, and the mix matters.

| | Internal lead (new Head of AI) | Outside consultant | Accidental owner ("you do AI too") |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who you answer to | Your line manager, who is usually the sponsor | The client sponsor, through a contract | Your existing boss, who may not be the sponsor |
| Authority | Whatever your job description and sponsor give you; it grows if you deliver | Only what the statement of work (SOW) says; you can recommend, not order | Usually none beyond your old role |
| Money | A budget line you may or may not control | Your fee is the budget; implementation money is the client's | None; every purchase is a favour |
| Time horizon | Years; you live with what you recommend | The engagement; you leave | Unclear; the AI work competes with your day job |
| How you exit | You don't; you hand over to operations or own the result | At the end of a phase, by acceptance of the deliverables | When someone else is appointed, or when it quietly stops |
| Main risk | Becoming the person who "owns AI" and therefore every request | Leaving a deck nobody uses; scope that grows without fees | Doing it in evenings until you burn out or it fails quietly |
| First document | Internal engagement charter | Proposal and SOW, then the charter | A one-page mandate request to your boss |

At Harbor & Pine you are the internal lead. You report to Michal (COO), who controls operations, customer service, logistics, purchasing and HR. Marketing, sales and the web store report to Yoav, the CEO — you have no line authority there (`company/org-chart.md`). That single fact will shape your scope in lesson 01.3.

If you are the **accidental owner**, your first move is different: before any AI work, write a half-page request to your manager naming the time you will spend (in hours a week or a percentage), what you will stop doing to make room, who decides when you disagree with another department, and when the arrangement will be reviewed. Without it you are running a programme with no mandate, and lesson 01.2 cannot start.

### Step 2 — If you are a consultant: sell phases you can exit

A consultant is paid to reduce the client's risk, so the engagement itself should be shaped to limit it. Sell **phases with an exit at each boundary**, not an open-ended "AI transformation":

1. **Phase 0 — Diagnose (3–5 weeks).** Mandate, scope, data-room read, interviews, shadow-AI census, inventory, baselines, findings memo, a ranked shortlist. Deliverable: a readiness assessment and a decision-ready case for one pilot. Exit: the client can stop here and keep everything.
2. **Phase 1 — Pilot (6–10 weeks).** One use case built and evaluated on the client's own data, with the minimum guardrails. Deliverable: working pilot, evaluation report, go/no-go recommendation.
3. **Phase 2 — Scale or stop (8–16 weeks).** Rollout, training, adoption measurement, handover to an operating rhythm.

Each phase has its own price and its own acceptance criteria, written before the work starts ("Phase 0 is complete when the sponsor has received and accepted documents A–F"). Ask on day one which shape the client thinks it bought: a client who expects a continuing partner and receives a phase-0 report feels abandoned; a client who expects a fixed deliverable and receives a monthly invoice feels milked.

**Pricing models.** Pick the one that matches how much is known:

| Model | How it works | Use it when | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed fee per phase | One price for defined deliverables | Scope and access are clear (a diagnostic with a known data room) | Scope creep is your loss: you need a change-request route (01.3) |
| Time and materials (T&M) | Day rate × days used, often with a cap | The work is uncertain (pilot build on data nobody has profiled) | The client carries the risk; give a cap and weekly burn reports |
| Retainer | Fixed monthly fee for a defined capacity (e.g. 4 days a month) | After a pilot: advisory, governance committee, office hours | Undefined deliverables; write what a month buys |
| Value-based or success fee | Part of the fee tied to a measured result | Only when the baseline is measured and the client controls the result | You rarely control adoption; a fee tied to "40% savings" rewards the wrong behaviour |

**Price to the client's approval rules, honestly.** Harbor & Pine's finance summary (`company/finance-summary.md`) sets the rules every proposal meets: up to ₪50,000 the department head and the CFO approve; ₪50,000–150,000 needs the CEO; above ₪150,000 in total over three years goes to the board; anything above ₪50,000 comes in the finance team's business-case template. A consultant quoting Phase 0 at 22 days × ₪5,500 (an illustrative day rate, not a market figure) = **₪121,000 plus VAT** lands in the CEO's band. Quote in ₪ and say "plus VAT": Israel's standard VAT rate is 18% (PwC tax summary, reviewed 29 June 2026), so the invoice will read ₪142,780. Ask Ronit (CFO) whether her thresholds are before or after VAT; do not guess.

**SOW outline.** A short SOW for Phase 0 has nine headings, and they are the same headings as the internal charter you will write in 01.2:

1. Background and the objective in the sponsor's words
2. Scope: in, out, and the change-request route
3. Deliverables, each with its acceptance criterion
4. Timeline and milestones
5. Client responsibilities (access, people's time, a named sponsor, decisions by date)
6. Fees, expenses, invoicing, VAT
7. Confidentiality and data handling (what data you receive, where it is kept, when it is deleted)
8. Assumptions and exclusions (no legal advice; no production changes; no vendor selection)
9. Exit: what the client keeps if it stops after this phase

The [engagement charter template](../../templates/engagement-charter.md) carries this outline in its last section.

### Step 3 — If you are internal: write the same thing anyway

Nobody will ask an internal lead for a SOW, and that is exactly why you need its content. Without written deliverables and acceptance criteria, an internal AI lead becomes the inbox for every AI idea in the company. Write the same nine headings as an **internal charter**, signed (an email reply is enough) by your sponsor. Your "fee" is your time and the budget you control; your "exit" is the handover of each piece of work to an owner in operations.

### Step 4 — Draw the advisory boundary on day one

In your first week people will ask you questions that sound operational and are really legal, security or commercial. Answer the framing and method questions yourself; route the rest to a named person, in writing, with the question phrased so they can answer it.

| Question you will hear | Answer yourself? | Route to |
|---|---|---|
| "Which use case should we start with?" | Yes — that is your job (module 3) | — |
| "Can agents paste customer emails into ChatGPT if they delete the name?" | No | Privacy counsel or the DPO; meanwhile an interim rule (01.6) |
| "Do we have to tell customers a bot answered them?" | No | Privacy counsel (the Privacy Protection Authority's April 2025 draft AI guidance says people should be told when they deal with a bot; it is still a draft) |
| "Is the vendor's data-processing addendum acceptable?" | You review the business terms (04.3) | Counsel signs off; procurement or finance owns the contract |
| "Can you see who is using ChatGPT, by name?" | No | Management decision with counsel; HR, because it concerns employees (01.6) |
| "If the bot works, can we let three agents go?" | No | CEO decision; HR and the company's labour lawyer for any process |
| "Is this proxy log safe to send you?" | Partly — ask for the minimum (01.5) | IT for the export; counsel if it identifies people |

**Israel first.** The Privacy Protection Law, 5741-1981 (חוק הגנת הפרטיות, התשמ"א-1981) was reformed by Amendment 13, in force since August 2025 (IAPP dates it 14 August 2025). Among other things, the amendment requires certain organizations to appoint a data protection officer: public bodies, data traders, organizations doing wide-scale systematic monitoring and those processing large volumes of sensitive data (Barnea Jaffa Lande's 2025 review). Whether a 120-person importer falls into one of those groups is a question for counsel, not for you. At Harbor & Pine the answer is already visible in the data room: IT's note says the privacy@ mailbox in the privacy notice goes to Avi, and nobody knows whether the 2025 changes require anything (`policies/data-retention-note.md`). So your route for privacy questions is the company's outside lawyer, through Ronit (CFO), until someone is appointed — and "who is our privacy contact?" goes on your list of open questions for Michal.

This course is not legal advice. Its job is to make you good at noticing a legal question and routing it well: who, what exactly, by when, and what you will do in the meantime.

### Step 5 — Write the entry note

End day one with a five-line note to your sponsor. It costs ten minutes and prevents the most common misunderstanding of all: the sponsor thinking you are doing one job while you are doing another.

> Michal — to confirm how I'm starting. Role: internal lead, reporting to you; I'll work through
> your departments directly and through Yoav's (marketing, sales) by request. This month I am
> diagnosing, not buying or building: no vendor commitments before the end of November. Legal,
> privacy and HR questions I'll route to the named owners below, not answer myself. I'll send the
> written objective after our meeting today and the 30-day plan by Thursday. Correct anything that's wrong.

## Watch out

- **Pricing a fixed fee on a data room you have not seen.** A diagnostic priced before you know whether exports exist is a guess. Price Phase 0 after a one-hour access check, or make the data-room read its own small fixed-fee step.
- **Splitting a phase to slip under an approval threshold.** Two ₪70,000 phases that are really one ₪140,000 piece of work, or one ₪160,000 piece cut in two to avoid the board, will be noticed by the CFO and cost you her trust. Size phases by what can be decided at their end, then show the total.
- **A success fee on someone else's promise.** "Paid on 40% savings" ties your income to a number the CEO chose before any data existed. Tie any variable fee to things you control (delivered, accepted, evaluated).
- **The hallway legal answer.** "Sure, just remove the names" sounds helpful and becomes company policy by lunch. Say "good question — I'll get you a real answer by Wednesday, and until then don't paste customer details", then route it.
- **The accidental owner who never asks.** If you were told to "also do AI", the cost of not writing a mandate request is invisible until month three. Write it in week one.
- **Forgetting whose side you are on.** A consultant serves the client organization, not the person who signed the order; an internal lead serves the company, not the CEO's announcement. When the two diverge (and at Harbor & Pine they may), the evidence decides, and you say so early.
