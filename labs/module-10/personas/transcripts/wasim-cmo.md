# Transcript — Dr. Wasim Haddad (CMO)

*Pre-run interview for lesson 10.2. Monday 16 November 2026, 13:30, Nazareth clinic, 45 minutes. The
interviewer had read the survey results, the incident log and the vendor decks. Read it as if you were
sitting in: note what the interviewer found out, what they missed, and which questions worked.*

---

**Interviewer:** Thank you for seeing me after a full list. I'm not here to ask whether AI is good. I'd
like to understand what makes a note hard to write at Galil.

**Wasim:** Good. Because the vendors only showed easy notes.

**Interviewer:** Take me through a hard one.

**Wasim:** An eighty-year-old woman in Nof HaGalil, Russian-speaking, with her son. The doctor speaks
Hebrew and some English. The son translates, the mother corrects him, they argue about which pill she
stopped. A nurse comes in to help. Ten minutes. The note is in Hebrew. Half the medical facts were said by
the son.

**Interviewer:** And what goes wrong in a note like that?

**Wasim:** Omissions. Not spelling. What the relative said about a medicine. An allergy mentioned in
passing. A brand name.

**Interviewer:** The vendors talk about accuracy. What would you want measured instead?

**Wasim:** How often a clinically relevant fact is missing or wrong in the note. How often it invents
something. And whether it tells the doctor where it was unsure.

**Interviewer:** Has a documentation tool ever caused a problem here?

**Wasim:** Yes. In the dictation trial last year. Two reports came to me. A dictated note said metformin
stopped when the doctor had said metoprolol; a pharmacist caught it. And a daughter told the doctor her
mother had stopped Eliquis; the dictated summary didn't have it; an anti-inflammatory was prescribed. The
family doctor caught it the next day.

**Interviewer:** Both caught by someone downstream.

**Wasim:** Both caught by luck, I'd say. Both about what someone said, not about typing.

**Interviewer:** Who interprets when the doctor doesn't speak the patient's language?

**Wasim:** Relatives, mostly. Nurses, a lot. Phone interpreters rarely. The nurses' time is the cost
nobody counts. Ask Suha.

**Interviewer:** I will. If we run a proof of concept, what would convince you?

**Wasim:** Our conditions. Three languages. Relatives. Noise. Not a studio. I can have my team write
scripts: ninety visits, thirty per language, each with a list of the facts the note must carry. Actors can
play them. And I can find six doctors to review the notes, two per language.

**Interviewer:** That's exactly what I hoped. Would you write the stop rule for a clinical pilot too?

**Wasim:** I'd insist on it.

**Interviewer:** What would you need from a vendor to sign off safety?

**Wasim:** A named clinical safety officer. A hazard log. Notice before they change the model, and a
re-test on our scripts. And incidents reported to us, not to their support desk.

**Interviewer:** One more. The survey comments: some doctors mention apps. Is that something you're aware
of?

**Wasim:** I read the comments.

**Interviewer:** Is it common?

**Wasim:** You'd have to ask Igor about the network.

**Interviewer:** Fair. Thank you. I'll send you a draft of the proof-of-concept protocol by the 25th.
