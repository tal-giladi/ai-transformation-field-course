# Transcript — Adv. Karim Nassar (senior associate, litigation)

*Pre-run interview for lesson 12.2. Monday 23 November 2026, 13:00, a meeting room with the door
closed, 45 minutes. Shmuel knows about the meeting. Read it as if you were sitting in: note what the
interviewer found out, what they missed, and which questions worked.*

---

**Interviewer:** Thanks for giving up your lunch. Before we start: nothing you say goes into my notes
with your name, and I'm not looking for anyone who did anything wrong. I'm trying to understand how
research actually gets done here.

**Karim:** OK.

**Interviewer:** Take the last research question you had. What did you do, step by step?

**Karim:** A question on agreed compensation in a construction contract. I start with my own folder,
then the databases, then I read the rulings themselves. Then a memo to Shmuel.

**Interviewer:** Your own folder, not Mikbatz?

**Karim:** Mikbatz says "current" on things that were reversed years ago. I keep about three hundred
rulings I trust, by issue.

**Interviewer:** How much of your week is research?

**Karim:** A quarter, easily. More for juniors.

**Interviewer:** The time entries show research codes on about six percent of associates' hours.

**Karim:** (smiles) RES gets cut, DRF survives. Everyone knows. So we write "drafting memo,
including checking case law".

**Interviewer:** Who cuts it?

**Karim:** The partners at billing review. Shmuel especially. He thinks the client shouldn't pay for
us learning.

**Interviewer:** When you check a citation properly, what does that involve?

**Karim:** Open the ruling. Read the paragraph that's cited, not the headnote. Check whether it was
appealed or reversed. Ten minutes a citation if you're careful.

**Interviewer:** And when it's eleven at night?

**Karim:** Nobody has ten minutes at eleven at night.

**Interviewer:** Has anyone here found an error in firm work that might have come from an AI tool?
Again, no names.

**Karim:** (long pause) Yes. Last month. A memo for a client, written overnight by a junior. One case
didn't exist anywhere. One had been reversed on appeal. One point said the opposite of the ruling.
It had already been approved to go out. I stopped it the next morning.

**Interviewer:** What happened after?

**Karim:** We fixed it together. It went out corrected. Nobody reported it. I won't tell you who.

**Interviewer:** I don't need to know who. What would have caught it earlier?

**Karim:** A rule that nobody signs a memo until every citation has been opened. And a bank we can
trust, so juniors don't go to a chatbot at midnight.

**Interviewer:** If the firm had a tool that searched only reviewed documents and showed the paragraph
next to each answer, would that help?

**Karim:** It would help if it says "not found" when it doesn't know. The danger is the confident
answer.

**Interviewer:** Would you help build a test for such a tool? Real questions, real answers?

**Karim:** If Shmuel agrees, yes. I have the questions in my head already.

**Interviewer:** How many questions would a fair test need, and in which languages?

**Karim:** Fifty, at least. Half Hebrew, because our rulings and memos are in Hebrew. And some where
the right answer is that the old rule was reversed.

**Interviewer:** What do juniors need most from all this?

**Karim:** Time to learn, and someone who checks with them, not after them.
